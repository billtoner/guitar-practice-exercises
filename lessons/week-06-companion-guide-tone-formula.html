The Guide-Tone Formula

Why the 3rd and 7th of one chord become the 7th and 3rd of the next — and why it's guaranteed.

Phase 2 · Connecting & phrasing. A reference sheet for Week 6 — Guide-Tone Lines. Part of the The Recall Gap 12-week plan.

What this sheet answers: for every ii–V–I we practice, what do the 3rd and 7th of one chord turn into in the NEXT chord — and why is that formula guaranteed to work? Once you see the mechanism, you stop memorizing lines and start predicting them.

2. The formula — what each note becomes in the next chord

The roles swap at every chord change:

This chord's……turns into the next chord's…What the note actually does
3rd7thstays put — same note, held (common tone)
7th3rdfalls a half-step

So at each change, one guide tone is glued in place and the other slides down a half-step. That's the whole trick.

3. Trace it — two nearly-motionless melodies

        Dm7        G7        Cmaj7
3rd →   F   ───→  (7th) F ──→ (3rd) E      F stays, then falls ½
7th →   C   ───→  (3rd) B ──→ (7th) B      C falls ½, then stays
  • F: 3rd of Dm7 → same F is the 7th of G7 → falls to E, the 3rd of Cmaj7.
  • C: 7th of Dm7 → falls to B, the 3rd of G7 → same B is the 7th of Cmaj7.

Two notes, almost no motion, and the whole progression is spelled out. That gliding, inevitable sound under Benson / Loeb / White lines is this.

4. Why the formula is guaranteed

Every progression in this plan is driven by roots moving down a fifth (D→G→C…), and our whole key cycle — C, F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭… — is that same cycle of fifths. That one fact forces the swap:

  • The 3rd of any chord is automatically the 7th slot of the chord a fifth below it. A 3rd sits ~4 semitones above its root; drop the root a fifth (7 semitones) and that same pitch is now ~11 semitones above the new root — i.e. a 7th. The note doesn't move; the chord moved underneath it.
  • The 7th of any chord lands one step above the 3rd slot of the chord a fifth below — so it resolves down into it.

The punchline: when the root falls a fifth, the 3rd and 7th trade jobs, and each note only has to move 0 or 1 semitone to fit the new chord. Smoothness isn't luck you memorize — it's arithmetic. Your ear just hears "0 or 1 semitone" as smooth.

5. The one wrinkle (so it generalizes to any progression)

Whether a guide tone stays or slides depends on the two chords' qualities:

Progression type3rd → 7th7th → 3rd
Diatonic ii–V–I (m7 → dom7 → maj7)common tone (stays)falls ½ step
All-dominant cycle (C7 → F7 → B♭7…)falls ½ stepfalls ½ step

In a pure dominant cycle both guide tones fall a half-step (the qualities no longer share a note), so you get a chromatic descent. Either way the rule holds: roots down a fifth → 3rd and 7th swap slots → each moves at most a half-step. The ii–V–I is just the smoothest special case, where half the notes don't move at all.

How to drill this (5 minutes)

  1. Call a ii–V–I in any key. Say the three chords out loud.
  2. Name the 3rd and 7th of the ii chord (Week 2 reflex).
  3. Predict the rest with the formula — don't recompute each chord: the 3rd is held into the next 7th; the 7th falls a half-step into the next 3rd.
  4. Check yourself against the pattern, then play both lines on the guitar.

When you can predict the line instead of working it out, you've internalized the formula — and that's the reflex Week 6 is really after.
